# Thinking Methods for Getting Unstuck

Choose one method that can change the current discussion. Show the new direction it produces before deciding whether to continue. Do not require the user to learn terminology or fill in a template.

## When the Problem Is Still Unclear: Return to a Concrete Situation

Translate the requested approach back into the outcome to improve. Find a real example, or one explicitly labeled hypothetical: who encounters the difficulty, how they handle it today, and where the cost occurs.

For example, "we need a knowledge base" does not distinguish difficulty finding material, distrust of its contents, and lack of maintenance. Establish which problem matters before changing search, verification, or ownership. Without user evidence, offer possibilities to confirm rather than claim that research has been done.

## When Every Idea Adds Features: Remove a Step

Try reducing information, operations, or systems instead of adding more. Could the outcome come from eliminating duplicate work, combining responsibilities, changing a default, or reusing existing tools?

Treat a smaller approach as a candidate to test, not an assumption that removal is always better. Preserve necessary capabilities, responsibilities, and confirmed user requirements.

## When Ideas Look Alike: Change One Main Variable

Change one dimension that affects the mechanism: who does the work, when it is triggered, who maintains the information, or whether a one-off service becomes a reusable tool. Put the result beside the original approach and explain the difference.

If this changes a hard constraint, present it separately as conditional exploration. Do not treat the constraint's removal as an agreed fact.

## When Convention Limits the Options: Borrow a Mechanism

Find a structurally similar problem in another setting. Borrow its coordination, feedback, allocation, or queuing mechanism rather than its surface appearance. Explain both the similarity and where the analogy fails.

For example, a queuing problem might benefit from reservations or scheduled time slots. The analogy does not prove that the target users will accept reservations; that needs separate validation.

## When You Cannot Find a Starting Point: Work Backward From Failure

Identify what would clearly defeat the goal, then infer conditions to avoid or monitor. If users cannot find the latest version, multiple publishing locations or unclear ownership might explain it. Generate directions from those possibilities rather than declaring a root cause.

During exploration, use this method to expand possibilities without eliminating each idea. During decision-making, examine which failure situation is worth preventing.

## When an Existing Idea Has Promise: Transform It Deliberately

Try replacing one component, combining adjacent uses, changing scale, or altering the sequence. Explain the problem each transformation addresses. Do not automatically apply every transformation or substitute more variants for meaningful new directions.

## When the Shortlist Is Stuck: Find Evidence That Separates the Options

Identify the premise that makes the candidates differ. Design a check capable of producing different decisions, rather than suggesting research in general.

For example, if the choice depends on whether an existing tool supports permission isolation, verify that boundary or run an authorized small test before writing complete architectures for both approaches.

## Limits on Combining Methods

One entry point is usually enough for a turn. If it produces no progress, explain the obstacle before changing methods or reframing the problem. Without new information, more techniques, candidates, or discussion are not necessarily progress.
